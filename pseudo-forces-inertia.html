dynamics — pseudo forces & inertia

The shove you feel that no one actually gave you

A bus brakes and you lurch forward with nothing touching your chest. A car corners and you're pressed against the door with no hand pushing you there. Nothing new is pushing you in either case — you're feeling your own inertia, but from inside a frame that's accelerating. To make the bookkeeping work out in that frame, physicists add a pseudo force: not real, but real enough to explain what you feel.

Inertia

An object's built-in resistance to a change in its motion. Mass is the measure of it — the more mass, the harder it is to speed up, slow down, or turn.

Pseudo force

A fictitious force introduced only inside a non-inertial (accelerating) frame, to make Newton's second law appear to hold there. It has no source and no reaction pair.

F_pseudo = −m·a_frame

Centrifugal force

The pseudo force felt by anything riding in a rotating frame — it points outward, away from the centre, and exists only for observers spinning along with the frame.

F_cf = m·ω²·r

The accelerating bus

A suitcase sits on the frictionless floor of a bus. Set the bus's acceleration and switch frames to see what "pushes" the suitcase in each one.

pseudo force (bus frame only) = 30.0 N

Ground frame. No real horizontal force acts on the suitcase, so it just stays put while the bus accelerates away beneath it — plain old inertia.

The spinning platform

A ball is tied by a string to the centre of a spinning platform. In the ground frame, the string's tension supplies the real centripetal force. In the platform's own spinning frame, the ball looks stationary only because an outward centrifugal pseudo force is added to balance the tension.

real tension, inward (ground frame) = 13.5 N     pseudo force, outward (platform frame) = 13.5 N

Practice

Work these by hand, then check yourself.

Problem 1 — concept check

A car turns sharply and a loose ball on the seat appears to fly outward, even though — from a view outside the car — nothing seems to be pushing it that way. What actually explains this?

Problem 2

A bus accelerates forward at 3 m/s². A 15 kg suitcase sits on its frictionless floor. Find the magnitude of the pseudo force felt by the suitcase in the bus's frame.

N
Show solution
The pseudo force has magnitude m·a_frame = 15 × 3 = 45 N, directed opposite the bus's acceleration — backward, toward the rear of the bus.

Problem 3

A car brakes, decelerating at 6 m/s². Find the magnitude of the pseudo force that seems to push a 65 kg passenger forward, in the car's frame.

N
Show solution
The car's acceleration points backward (it's slowing down), so the pseudo force points the opposite way — forward — with magnitude 65 × 6 = 390 N. That's the "shove" that throws you toward the windshield when the driver brakes hard.

Problem 4

A 2 kg object sits on a platform rotating at ω = 4 rad/s, at a radius of 1.5 m from the centre. Find the real centripetal force needed (ground frame) and the centrifugal pseudo force felt (rotating frame) — both share the same magnitude.

N
Show solution
Both come from the same expression: F = mω²r = 2 × 4² × 1.5 = 48 N. In the ground frame this is the real, inward tension needed to keep the object turning. In the platform's own spinning frame, an equal outward pseudo force is added so the (apparently stationary) object still balances to zero net force.
