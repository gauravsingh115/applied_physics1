01 — Fundamental (base) units

The seven that don't depend on anything else

A fundamental unit — also called a base unit — measures a quantity that can't be defined in terms of any other unit. It stands on its own. SI recognizes seven of them, one per base physical quantity, and every other unit in physics, chemistry, and engineering is built by multiplying and dividing these seven.

Until 2019, some were tied to physical objects — a platinum-iridium cylinder kept in a vault outside Paris defined the kilogram. Today all seven are defined by fixing the exact numerical value of a constant of nature.

02 — Derived units

Combinations that measure everything else

A derived unit measures any quantity that isn't one of the seven base quantities, and it's built by multiplying and dividing base units, raised to whatever power the physics demands. Speed is metres per second. Acceleration is metres per second, per second. Some combinations come up so often that SI gives them their own name and symbol — a newton isn't a new kind of unit, it's just kg·m·s⁻² with a nicer name.

03 — Dimensions

What kind of quantity, not what unit

A dimension describes what kind of quantity something is — length, mass, time — independent of the particular unit used to measure it. A length has dimension L whether you write it in metres, feet, or light-years; the unit changes, the dimension never does.

Physicists write a quantity's dimensional formula in brackets, using the seven base symbols raised to whatever power the definition demands. Force is [M L T⁻²], because F = ma multiplies a mass by a length divided by a time squared. Two sides of a real physical equation must always match, dimension for dimension — a rule called dimensional homogeneity, and a fast way to catch a wrong formula before you've even plugged in numbers.

QuantityDefining relationDimensional formula
Velocityv = d / t[L T⁻¹]
Accelerationa = v / t[L T⁻²]
ForceF = m · a[M L T⁻²]
EnergyE = F · d[M L² T⁻²]
Pressurep = F / A[M L⁻¹ T⁻²]
Densityρ = m / V[M L⁻³]
Units vs. dimensions, in one line: the dimension tells you the kind of quantity (a length); the unit tells you the scale you're measuring it on (metres, or miles). Different units, same dimension.
