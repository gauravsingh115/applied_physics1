Jerk: the rudest quantity in kinematics

Velocity is speed with an opinion about direction. Acceleration is velocity changing its mind. Jerk is acceleration changing its mind about changing its mind — and it's the reason a smooth elevator ride is boring and a bad one ends up on your shirt.

Physicists named it "jerk" because that's genuinely what it feels like. No committee. No vote. Just vibes and a coffee stain.

What it actually is

Jerk is the rate of change of acceleration — the third derivative of position. You never meet it in intro problems because most of them politely assume acceleration snaps on instantly. It doesn't, and something has to describe how "instantly" it snaps.

jerk = Δacceleration ⁄ Δtime
position x → velocity dx/dt → acceleration d²x/dt²
→ jerk d³x/dt³ ← you are here

Why anyone bothers

Your inner ear doesn't measure acceleration directly — it measures how suddenly it starts. That's jerk. It's why a rocket launch and a gentle elevator can both hit high acceleration, but only one of them is described as "smooth."

Elevator engineers, train designers, and roller coaster builders spend real budget limiting jerk on purpose. Not because high acceleration scares your body — because sudden acceleration does.

Practice

Work these by hand, then check yourself. No actual coffee was harmed in the making of these problems.

Problem 1 — concept check

Two rides reach the same top speed in the same time. Ride A ramps its acceleration up smoothly. Ride B's acceleration switches on instantly, like a bad rideshare driver flooring it from a red light. Which ride is more likely to spill your coffee, and why?

Problem 2

An elevator's acceleration ramps at a constant rate from 0 to 1.2 m/s² over 0.6 s. Find its jerk during that ramp.

m/s³
Show solution
Jerk is the change in acceleration over time: (1.2 − 0) ⁄ 0.6 = 2 m/s³. This is roughly what a genuinely comfortable elevator aims for.

Problem 4

A very patient train's acceleration ramps linearly from 0 up to 0.8 m/s² over 4 s, holds there a while, then ramps back down to 0 over another 4 s. What is the jerk during the ramp-up?

m/s³
Show solution
0.8 ⁄ 4 = 0.2 m/s³ during ramp-up. During ramp-down it's the same magnitude but negative, −0.2 m/s³ — acceleration is now decreasing at a steady rate instead of increasing.
