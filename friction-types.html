dynamics — friction & types

The force that only shows up when you ask it to

Friction is a contact force that opposes relative sliding — or the tendency to slide — between two touching surfaces. Unlike gravity, it doesn't have a fixed value it always pushes with; it reacts, supplying just enough force to resist motion, right up until it can't anymore. That reactive behaviour is what makes friction feel strange at first, and it's also what makes it worth studying carefully. Push the block below and watch friction respond in real time.

Four kinds of friction

Every case of "something resisting relative motion" falls into one of these four categories.

Static

Resists the start of sliding. Adjusts to match whatever pushes the object, up to a maximum — beyond that, it can no longer keep up.

f_s ≤ μ_s N

Kinetic

Opposes surfaces that are already sliding. Roughly constant in the idealised model, and usually a bit smaller than the maximum static value.

f_k = μ_k N

Rolling

Resists a wheel or ball as it rolls rather than slides. Usually much smaller than sliding friction between the same materials.

μ_roll ≪ μ_k

Fluid

Drag from moving through a liquid or gas — air resistance, water resistance. Grows with speed rather than staying constant.

grows with speed

Static vs. kinetic: watch the switch happen

Slide the applied force up slowly. Friction matches it exactly at first — that's static friction protecting the block from moving. Once the applied force crosses the maximum static friction can supply, the block breaks free and kinetic friction takes over, usually at a slightly lower, constant value.

Problem 2

A 10 kg box has μ_s = 0.5 and μ_k = 0.35 with the floor. A horizontal force of 40 N is applied. Determine whether the box moves, and find the friction force acting on it.

Show solution
Maximum static friction is μ_s·m·g = 0.5 × 10 × 9.8 = 49 N. The applied force, 40 N, is less than this, so the box stays put — static friction simply matches the push: f = 40 N.

Problem 3

The same box now has the applied force increased to 60 N — enough to overcome the maximum static friction. Find the kinetic friction force and the box's acceleration.

Once sliding, friction switches to the kinetic value: f_k = μ_k·m·g = 0.35 × 10 × 9.8 = 34.3 N. Net force is 60 − 34.3 = 25.7 N, so a = 25.7 / 10 = 2.57 m/s².

Problem 4

A 25 kg crate can either be dragged (μ_k = 0.4) or rolled on a dolly (μ_roll = 0.02) across the same floor. Find the friction force resisting each method, and how many times larger the sliding friction is compared to the rolling friction.

Show solution
Sliding: f_k = 0.4 × 25 × 9.8 = 98 N. Rolling: f_roll = 0.02 × 25 × 9.8 = 4.9 N. The ratio is 98 / 4.9 = 20 — the dolly needs twenty times less force, which is exactly why wheels were such a good idea.
