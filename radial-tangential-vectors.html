circular motion — radial & tangential vectors

A coordinate frame that travels with the particle

x̂ and ŷ never change direction. For circular motion, it's often far more useful to build a frame that does: r̂ always points outward from the center to the particle, and θ̂ always points perpendicular to that, in the direction of increasing angle. Any vector — velocity, acceleration, an applied force — can be split into how much of it points along r̂ and how much points along θ̂.

Radial unit vector, r̂

Points from the center straight out to the particle. Its direction depends entirely on θ — it rotates right along with the particle.

r̂ = (cosθ, sinθ)

Tangential unit vector, θ̂

Perpendicular to r̂, pointing in the direction of increasing θ. For an object moving purely along the circle, this is the direction of its velocity.

θ̂ = (−sinθ, cosθ)

A rotating frame

Unlike x̂ and ŷ, r̂ and θ̂ themselves change direction as θ changes — that single fact is what makes circular motion's calculus distinctive.

A particle sits at angular position θ = 30°. Find the (x, y) components of its tangential unit vector, θ̂ = (−sinθ, cosθ).

Show solution
θ̂ = (−sin30°, cos30°) = (−0.5, 0.866).

Problem 3

A vector of magnitude 8 N points in direction φ = 200°, evaluated at a particle located at θ = 140°. Find its radial and tangential components.

Show solution
φ − θ = 60°, so V_r = 8 cos60° = 4 N and V_t = 8 sin60° ≈ 6.93 N.

Problem 4

A particle at radius 3 m has zero radial velocity component (it stays on the circle) and a tangential velocity component of 12 m/s. Find its angular speed.

rad/s
Show solution
Tangential speed relates to angular speed by v_t = rω, so ω = v_t/r = 12/3 = 4 rad/s.
