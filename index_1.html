Part 1 · Differentiation

What is a derivative? Zoom in until the curve looks straight.

Geometric

Pick two points on a curve and the slope between them is an average rate of change — that's the secant line. Now slide the second point toward the first. The secant keeps rotating until, in the limit, it settles onto the tangent line: the instantaneous rate of change at a single point. That limit is the derivative.

Physical

Same idea, different story: velocity is a derivative

A car's position s(t) traces a curve on a position-vs-time graph. Average velocity over an interval is just a secant slope again — displacement over elapsed time. Instantaneous velocity, the number on the speedometer right now, is the tangent slope: s′(t).

Scenario

A cart's position is s(t) = t² metres, t in seconds (it's accelerating — think of it rolling down a ramp). The track below the graph shows where the cart actually is.

average velocity = [s(t₀+Δt) − s(t₀)] / Δt instantaneous velocity = s′(t₀) = 2t₀

This is the same limiting process as the geometric picture above — only now the slope literally means "how fast is this thing moving," which is why engineers care about derivatives at all.

Now the shortcuts, seen rather than memorized

Think of the product u(x)·v(x) as the area of a rectangle with width u and height v. Nudge x forward a little: the rectangle grows by a top strip (u·Δv) plus a side strip (v·Δu) — the tiny corner (Δu·Δv) vanishes faster than the rest as Δx→0.

Example: g(x) = x², f(u) = sin(u), so h(x) = f(g(x)) = sin(x²). A nudge in x gets stretched by g′(x) on its way through g, then stretched again by f′(g(x)) on its way through f. Two stretches in a row multiply.

(f∘g)′(x) = f′(g(x))·g′(x)

The aqua tangent line on h(x) = sin(x²) always has exactly this slope — the abstract pipeline and the geometric tangent agree.

Part 2 · Integration

What is an integral? Fill the area with thinner and thinner rectangles.

Geometric

To find the area under a curve, approximate it with rectangles — easy shapes we already know how to measure. A few fat rectangles are a rough guess. As you add more, thinner ones, the guess tightens. In the limit of infinitely many infinitely thin rectangles, the sum is the exact area — that limit is the definite integral.

Physical

Same idea, different story: area under velocity is distance

Flip the earlier motion example around. Now we're handed a velocity-vs-time graph and asked how far the object travelled. Chop time into thin slices; over each slice velocity is roughly constant, so distance ≈ velocity × Δt — the area of a thin strip. Add the strips.

Distance travelled = area

Scenario

A drone's velocity is v(t) = 3 − 0.5t m/s (decelerating), t in seconds. The shaded region is the distance covered so far — as t sweeps forward, watch the drone on the track move by exactly the shaded area, not the current velocity.

distance(t) = ∫0t v(τ) dτ

This is the mirror image of the differentiation motion example: there we went position → velocity by differentiating; here we go velocity → distance by integrating (accumulating). That mirror-image relationship is exactly the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, coming up.

Rules refresher

Integration rules: mostly differentiation in reverse

Every rule below is a differentiation rule read right-to-left. Recognizing that saves you from re-memorizing anything.

∫xn dx = xn+1/(n+1) + C

Every one of the parallel aqua curves below has exactly the same derivative, shown in blue underneath — because a vertical shift doesn't change slope anywhere. That's why the "+C" matters: the antiderivative isn't one curve, it's a whole family. Drag C to slide your chosen curve through the family.

∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx
← recognize u=g(x) →
∫ cos(u) du

The integrand 2x·cos(x²) is exactly a chain-rule output: with u = g(x) = x², du = 2x·dx, so the integral becomes ∫cos(u)du = sin(u)+C = sin(x²)+C — the reverse of the chain-rule pipeline from the differentiation rules panel.

∫ f′(g(x))·g′(x) dx = f(g(x)) + C

The blue accumulated area (computed by literally summing thin strips) should match the aqua closed-form value at every x — a nice numerical check that u-substitution really does work.

Part 3 · The bridge

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: they undo each other

Define an accumulation function A(x) = the running area under f from a fixed start point up to x. As x sweeps rightward, A(x) is being built up in real time by the integral. Now watch A(x)'s own tangent slope — it always equals the height of f at that same x.

f(x) — height
—
A′(x) — slope of accumulation
—

Reading the diagram

Top: f(x) = 0.6x² − x + 1.6 with the running shaded area A(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t)dt.
Bottom: A(x) itself being traced out, with its tangent line at the current x.

A(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt  ⟹  A′(x) = f(x)

Notice the two readouts above always match. That single fact — differentiating an accumulated area gives back the original function — is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. It's why "integration" and "antidifferentiation" are taught as the same operation: finding an antiderivative is how we evaluate a definite integral in practice, instead of summing infinitely many strips by hand.
