dynamics — forces & types

What a force actually does, and the shapes it comes in

A force is any push or pull that can change how something moves — speed it up, slow it down, or bend its path. Every force needs a source and a target, and it always comes with a direction, which is why forces are vectors, not plain numbers. It might feel like there are countless different kinds of forces out there — a hand pushing a box, a magnet grabbing a paperclip, a nucleus holding itself together — but physics traces every single one of them back to just four fundamental forces of nature.

The four forces of nature

Every interaction in the universe, from planets orbiting stars to a proton staying glued together, ultimately runs on one of these four. They differ enormously in strength and reach.

Gravitational

Attracts every pair of masses. By far the weakest of the four, but always attractive and reaches forever — which is why it's the one that shapes planets, stars, and galaxies.

F = Gm₁m₂ / r²
range: infinite

Electromagnetic

Acts between charged particles — attractive or repulsive. Also infinite range, and far stronger than gravity. It's responsible for light, chemistry, and almost every force you can actually feel.

F ∝ q₁q₂ / r²
range: infinite

Strong nuclear

Binds protons and neutrons together inside the nucleus. The strongest force by far — but its grip barely reaches past the nucleus itself.

range: ~10⁻¹⁵ m

Weak nuclear

Lets one type of particle transform into another, driving radioactive decay. Short-ranged and weaker than the strong and electromagnetic forces, but still far stronger than gravity.

range: ~10⁻¹⁸ m

Everyday forces, sorted

Push a box, tie a knot, sit on a chair — at human scale you only ever really notice two of the four fundamental forces. Gravity keeps everything pinned down, and almost everything else you can touch — friction, tension, the normal force, a hand pushing a box — is the electromagnetic force in disguise: atoms resisting being squeezed together or pulled apart. That split gives mechanics its own everyday way of sorting forces.

Contact force

Needs the two objects to be touching: a hand pushing a box, a rope pulling a sled, friction dragging a sliding crate, a table holding up a book.

Non-contact force

Acts across a gap, no touching required: gravity pulling you toward Earth, a magnet grabbing a paperclip, static charge tugging at your hair.

Net force

Add every force on an object as vectors and what's left over is the net force — the only thing that decides whether, and how, the object accelerates.

F_net = ΣF

A 4 kg box is pushed with a horizontal force of 18 N. The floor exerts a kinetic friction force of 6 N on it. Find the net force on the box and its acceleration.

Net force is the applied force minus the opposing friction: 18 − 6 = 12 N. Newton's second law then gives the acceleration: a = F_net / m = 12 / 4 = 3 m/s², in the direction of the push.

These two forces are perpendicular, so Pythagoras gives the size of the resultant: √(30² + 40²) = √2500 = 50 N. The direction comes from tan⁻¹(40/30) ≈ 53.1° north of east.

Weight is gravity's pull: W = mg = 12 × 9.8 = 117.6 N. The crate isn't accelerating vertically, so the net vertical force is zero — the table's normal force must exactly cancel the weight: N = 117.6 N.
